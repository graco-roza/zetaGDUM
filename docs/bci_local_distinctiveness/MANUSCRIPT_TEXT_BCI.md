## Methods (empirical demonstration)
We analysed the 50-ha Barro Colorado Island forest dynamics plot (Condit et al. 2019), at the resolution of Legendre and Condit (2019): 1250 quadrats of 20 m × 20 m. The principal analysis uses the 1985 census (census 2), and the 2015 census (census 8) serves as a temporal comparison. Abundances are counts of live trees (status "A", dbh ≥ 10 mm; one record per tree), which reproduces the totals reported by Legendre and Condit (244,015 individuals in 1990; net swamp gain of 1,354 and non-swamp loss of 35,697 individuals between 1985 and 2015). Quadrats were assigned to the seven habitat classes of the Harms et al. (2001) map (swamp, 30 quadrats; stream; slope; low and high plateau; young forest; mixed). Conventional local contributions to beta diversity (LCBD) were computed from Hellinger-transformed abundances (Legendre and De Cáceres 2013) and tested with 99,999 permutations and Holm correction over the 1250 quadrats. We computed, with zetaGDUM, the exact local contribution to zeta dissimilarity (LCZD) of each quadrat from relative abundances and from occurrences at orders 2 to 640, and the local retention profiles (conditional shared composition of the sets of k quadrats that contain the focal quadrat, k = 1 to 1250). The characteristic retention order T is the sum of the retention profile over all orders, and the conditional retention order T+ = 1 + (T − 1)/ζ2|i is the retained order count conditional on sharing at order two. Species contributions to local dissimilarity were obtained from the same exact conditional expectations and split into an occupancy component (species absent from some sets of sites) and an abundance-heterogeneity component (shared species with unequal abundances). We use "regional representativeness" only descriptively, for the extent to which a quadrat's composition is retained across multisite sets; it is not a measure of conservation value or irreplaceability.

## Results
All comparisons pair like with like: abundance LCBD (Hellinger transformation of abundances) is compared with relative-abundance LCZD and retention, and occurrence LCBD (the same Hellinger transformation applied to presence-absence) with incidence LCZD and occurrence retention.

**LCBD reproduces the published pattern in both representations.** In 1985, 96 quadrats had abundance LCBD significant at 0.05, 28 of them in the 30 swamp quadrats; after Holm correction 14 remained, 12 of them in the swamp. In 2015, 126 quadrats were significant (26 in the swamp) but only two remained after correction, both on slopes, so no swamp quadrat retained a significant LCBD, as reported by Legendre and Condit. Occurrence LCBD was moderately correlated with abundance LCBD (Spearman ρ = 0.57 in 1985, 0.60 in 2015) and also singled out the swamp: 30 of 30 swamp quadrats were significant at 0.05 in 1985 and 26 in 2015; after Holm correction 24 quadrats remained significant in 1985 (18 in the swamp) and, unlike in the abundance analysis, 12 in 2015 (7 in the swamp), so swamp distinctiveness in occurrence persisted whereas its abundance signal was lost. Mean swamp LCBD fell between censuses from 1.78 to 1.33 × 10⁻³ (abundance) and from 1.24 to 1.05 × 10⁻³ (occurrence), while that of other habitats did not change.

**Distinctiveness persists across orders when abundance is used.** Abundance LCZD remained correlated with abundance LCBD (ρ = 0.93 at order 2, 0.62 at order 80, 0.53 at order 320), and 93% of swamp quadrats were in the top decile at order 2 and 83% at order 80. Occurrence LCBD was only weakly related to incidence LCZD at order 2 (ρ = 0.30) and negatively at higher orders (ρ = −0.37 at order 80), and the mean percentile of swamp quadrats in incidence LCZD declined from 0.80 at order 2 to 0.53 at order 80 and 0.12 at order 640 (Fig. S1). Incidence LCZD is expressed in richness units whereas occurrence LCBD standardises by richness, so part of this divergence is a difference in scaling.

**Characteristic retention follows pairwise distinctiveness in abundance, but not in occurrence.** Abundance-based T was strongly and negatively related to abundance LCBD (ρ = −0.86; a smooth function of LCBD explained 78% of its variance), because T is dominated by the shared composition at order two. Occurrence-based T was positively, not negatively, related to occurrence LCBD (ρ = 0.39), and LCBD explained only 20% of its variance (Fig. 4D). Quadrats with LCBD within 2% differed by a median of 0.08 abundance-based orders (95th percentile 0.50) but by 1.6 occurrence-based orders (95th percentile 5.1). Quadrats matched on both LCBDs (within 3%; 10,671 pairs) differed by a median of 0.06 abundance orders but 1.5 occurrence orders (95th percentile 4.6). An example (Fig. 4C): a slope quadrat with 73 species and a mixed-habitat quadrat with 28 species had abundance LCBD of 0.98 and 0.99 × 10⁻³, occurrence LCBD of 1.00 and 1.01 × 10⁻³ and indistinguishable abundance retention profiles (T = 9.0 and 9.0), yet occurrence-based T of 9.1 and 21.6. The difference lies in the occupancy of the species shared with other quadrats, not in relative abundance.

**Distinctiveness does not imply low retention.** Among the 63 quadrats in the highest 5% of occurrence LCBD, 84% had occurrence-based T above the plot median (median percentile 0.82), and only one (a swamp quadrat) was in the lowest decile. Across the plot, conditional retention T+ was strongly and positively related to occurrence LCBD (ρ = 0.74) and to abundance LCBD (ρ = 0.84). In abundance, in contrast, distinctive quadrats lost most of their shared composition at order two, so low T accompanied high LCBD (swamp median 6.4 versus 9.0), but the part that remained was retained for longer (T+ 19.7 versus 17.7). Within the swamp, T and T+ were uncorrelated (ρ = −0.08, versus −0.76 elsewhere).

**The swamp is distinctive in both representations but unrepresentative only in abundance.** In 1985 swamp quadrats had higher abundance LCBD (Cliff's δ = 0.97) and occurrence LCBD (δ = 0.98) than other habitats, lower abundance-based T (6.4 versus 9.0; δ = −0.91), but higher occurrence-based T (14.6 versus 12.1; δ = 0.57) and T+ (32.5 versus 23.0; δ = 0.88). They held far fewer individuals (95 versus 193) and species (35 versus 54) per quadrat, so the swamp is a species-poor subset dominated by few species in unusual proportions rather than a quadrat set with a unique species pool. Of the 63 quadrats in the highest 5% of abundance LCBD, 26 were swamp; the other 37 had occurrence-based T spanning almost the whole range, and a slope quadrat (x004_y023, LCBD in the top 1%) had the highest abundance-based T+ of the plot.

**Species.** Restricted occupancy and numerical dominance contribute differently (Fig. S4). Bactris major, recorded in 57 quadrats of the plot but in 63% of swamp quadrats, contributed 0.095 of the swamp quadrats' mean expected dissimilarity at order 2 (0.71), almost all through restricted occupancy. Faramea occidentalis, present in all 1250 quadrats and abundant in the swamp (12% versus 10% of individuals), contributed 0.045 at order two and 0.105 at order 40, through abundance heterogeneity (99% and 86%, respectively). The conspicuous swamp indicator Elaeis oleifera ranked only 23rd. The abundance-heterogeneity share of local dissimilarity was lowest in the swamp (0.33 at order 2 versus 0.38-0.57 in other habitats) and decreased with order in all habitats (0.15-0.28 at order 40), so differentiation at higher orders is increasingly an occupancy phenomenon.

**Temporal comparison (Fig. S3).** Between 1985 and 2015 the swamp gained 11 species and 35 individuals per quadrat on average (medians) whereas other habitats lost individuals. Its abundance-based T rose by 2.0 orders (0.7-0.8 elsewhere) and its LCBD fell, so it became less distinctive and more representative in abundance, although it remained near the bottom of the plot ranking (percentile 0.06 to 0.08). Its occurrence-based T rose by only 1.6 orders (3.5-4.7 elsewhere), losing its earlier above-average occurrence retention (mean percentile 0.78 to 0.48). The number of swamp quadrats in the top 5% of LCBD fell from 26 to 20 (abundance) and from 26 to 19 (occurrence). Quadrat-level patterns were moderately stable between censuses (ρ = 0.66 for abundance LCBD, 0.53 for occurrence LCBD, 0.63 for abundance-based T and 0.39 for occurrence-based T; within the swamp 0.75, 0.36, 0.81 and 0.12).

## Discussion paragraph
Pairwise LCBD identifies the swamp quadrats as the most distinctive of the plot, as in Legendre and Condit (2019). The multisite local metrics add information that cannot be read from LCBD alone. First, in abundance data, characteristic retention is mostly a re-expression of pairwise distinctiveness; the additional information is in the conditional retention order, which shows that the shared composition of distinctive quadrats is retained more persistently across orders, as expected if the shared part is made of widespread dominant species. Second, with occurrence data retention is only weakly and, unexpectedly, positively related to LCBD: quadrats with indistinguishable abundance and occurrence LCBD differ by up to twelve orders in how widely their species are shared, which reflects the occupancy distribution of their species, and most distinctive quadrats are more, not less, retained. Third, the two representations disagree about where distinctive quadrats are at higher orders, and the abundance-based picture, driven by a few dominant species in unusual proportions, is the more persistent. Fourth, change over time can be decomposed: the swamp lost its significant abundance LCBD but kept a significant occurrence LCBD in 2015, and it became less distinct in abundance without becoming more representative in occurrence, which pairwise distinctiveness in a single representation cannot show. Caveats are that the swamp quadrats contain half as many individuals as other quadrats, so part of their distinctiveness may reflect sampling variance; that retention orders depend on the plot extent and quadrat size; and that regional representativeness is not conservation value or irreplaceability: a quadrat with low retention can be ordinary in its species pool, and a highly retentive quadrat can contain locally important species.

## Figure 4 caption
**Figure 4. Local compositional distinctiveness and regional representativeness in the Barro Colorado Island plot, 1985 census.** Abundance (upper) and occurrence (lower) representations are shown side by side so that each comparison pairs like with like. (A) Conventional local contribution to beta diversity (LCBD; Hellinger-transformed abundances, and Hellinger-transformed presence-absence) of the 1250 20 m × 20 m quadrats; orange outlines mark the 30 swamp quadrats and dots the quadrats with Holm-significant LCBD (99,999 permutations). (B) Characteristic retention order T, the sum of the conditional zeta profile over all orders, from relative abundance (upper) and occurrence (lower); dark quadrats retain shared composition across fewer orders in the upper map and across more orders in the lower map (colour scales differ). (C) Retention profiles (conditional shared composition against zeta order, log axis) of two quadrats matched on both LCBDs (within 1.2%) with almost identical abundance retention but occurrence-based T of 9.1 and 21.6; grey: regional median and 5-95% range. (D) LCBD against characteristic retention order T in each representation (Spearman ρ = −0.86 for abundance, 0.39 for occurrence), coloured by habitat; swamp quadrats lie below the abundance trend and span the upper range of occurrence retention.
