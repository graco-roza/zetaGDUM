## Methods (empirical demonstration)
We analysed the 50-ha Barro Colorado Island forest dynamics plot (Condit et al. 2019), at the resolution of Legendre and Condit (2019): 1250 quadrats of 20 m × 20 m. The principal analysis uses the 1985 census (census 2), and the 2015 census (census 8) serves as a temporal comparison. Abundances are counts of live trees (status "A", dbh ≥ 10 mm; one record per tree), which reproduces the totals reported by Legendre and Condit (244,015 individuals in 1990; net swamp gain of 1,354 and non-swamp loss of 35,697 individuals between 1985 and 2015). Quadrats were assigned to the seven habitat classes of the Harms et al. (2001) map (swamp, 30 quadrats; stream; slope; low and high plateau; young forest; mixed). Conventional local contributions to beta diversity (LCBD) were computed from Hellinger-transformed abundances (Legendre and De Cáceres 2013) and tested with 99,999 permutations and Holm correction over the 1250 quadrats. We computed, with zetaGDUM, the exact local contribution to zeta dissimilarity (LCZD) of each quadrat from relative abundances and from occurrences at orders 2 to 640, and the local retention profiles (conditional shared composition of the sets of k quadrats that contain the focal quadrat, k = 1 to 1250). The characteristic retention order T is the sum of the retention profile over all orders, and the conditional retention order T+ = 1 + (T − 1)/ζ2|i is the retained order count conditional on sharing at order two. Species contributions to local dissimilarity were obtained from the same exact conditional expectations and split into an occupancy component (species absent from some sets of sites) and an abundance-heterogeneity component (shared species with unequal abundances). We use "regional representativeness" only descriptively, for the extent to which a quadrat's composition is retained across multisite sets; it is not a measure of conservation value or irreplaceability.

## Results
**LCBD reproduces the published pattern.** In 1985, 96 quadrats had LCBD significant at 0.05, 28 of them in the 30 swamp quadrats; after Holm correction 14 remained, 12 of them in the swamp. In 2015, 126 quadrats were significant at 0.05 (26 in the swamp) but only two remained after Holm correction, both on slopes, so no swamp quadrat retained a significant LCBD, as reported by Legendre and Condit. Mean swamp LCBD fell from 1.78 × 10⁻³ to 1.33 × 10⁻³ while that of other habitats was unchanged (0.78 → 0.79 × 10⁻³).

**Distinctiveness persists across orders when abundance is used, but not when only occurrence is used.** Abundance-based LCZD remained correlated with LCBD (Spearman ρ = 0.93 at order 2, 0.62 at order 80, 0.53 at order 320), and the swamp stayed in the top decile (93% of swamp quadrats at order 2, 83% at order 80). Incidence-based LCZD was only weakly related to LCBD (ρ = 0.44 at order 2, 0.06 at order 80), and the mean percentile of swamp quadrats declined from 0.80 at order 2 to 0.53 at order 80 and 0.12 at order 640 (Fig. S1). The identity of the most distinctive quadrats therefore depends on whether abundance is considered, and the abundance signal survives to much higher orders (Q1, Q4).

**Characteristic retention largely mirrors LCBD when abundance is used; it does not when occurrence is used.** Abundance-based T was strongly and negatively related to LCBD (ρ = −0.86; a smooth function of LCBD explained 78% of its variance), because T is dominated by the shared composition at order two. Occurrence-based T was unrelated to LCBD (ρ = −0.03; 3% of variance explained, Fig. 4D). Quadrats with LCBD within 2% of each other differed by a median of only 0.08 abundance-based orders, whereas occurrence-based T varied over 6.4 orders between the 5th and 95th percentile of the plot and left a residual SD of about two orders after accounting for LCBD. Concrete examples (Fig. 4C, S5): two swamp quadrats with LCBD 1.55 and 1.56 × 10⁻³ had occurrence-based T of 11.1 and 19.6, and a swamp and a slope quadrat with LCBD 1.39 and 1.41 × 10⁻³ had T = 20.2 and 12.3, although the abundance-based retention profiles of the second pair were almost identical. These differences arise from which and how widely distributed the species shared by the quadrat are (their occupancy), not from relative abundance.

**Conditional retention separates the swamp from other distinctive quadrats.** Among quadrats with high pairwise distinctiveness, T+ (abundance) was positively related to LCBD (ρ = 0.84, 61% of variance explained by LCBD), so, once the large loss at order two is conditioned out, distinctive quadrats retained the remaining shared composition longer than ordinary ones (swamp median 19.7 versus 17.7 orders). Within the swamp T and T+ were uncorrelated (ρ = −0.08, versus −0.76 in other habitats; Fig. S2C), so the two quantities carry different information: T measures how much composition is shared, T+ how persistently the shared part is retained.

**The swamp is distinctive and unrepresentative in abundance, but not in occurrence.** In 1985 swamp quadrats had higher LCBD (median 1.8 versus 0.7 × 10⁻³; Cliff's δ = 0.97) and lower abundance-based T (6.4 versus 9.0; δ = −0.91), but higher occurrence-based T (14.6 versus 12.1; δ = 0.57) and occurrence-based T+ (32.5 versus 23.0; δ = 0.88). They held far fewer individuals (95 versus 193) and species (35 versus 54) per quadrat. The swamp is thus a species-poor subset dominated by few species in unusual proportions, rather than a quadrat set with a unique species pool. Of the 63 quadrats in the highest 5% of LCBD, 26 were swamp; the other 37 (mostly slope and low-plateau quadrats) had occurrence-based T spanning almost the whole range (from the lowest 0.1% to the top 2% of the plot), and one of them (slope quadrat x004_y023, LCBD in the top 1%) had the highest abundance-based T+ in the plot.

**Species.** Restricted occupancy and numerical dominance contribute differently (Fig. S4). Bactris major, recorded in 57 quadrats of the plot but in 63% of swamp quadrats, contributed 0.095 of the swamp quadrats' mean expected dissimilarity at order 2 (0.71), almost all through restricted occupancy. Faramea occidentalis, present in all 1250 quadrats and abundant in the swamp (12% versus 10% of individuals), contributed 0.045 at order two and 0.105 at order 40, through abundance heterogeneity (99% and 86%, respectively). The abundance-heterogeneity share of local dissimilarity was lowest in the swamp (0.33 at order 2 versus 0.38–0.57 in other habitats) and decreased with order in all habitats (0.15–0.28 at order 40), so differentiation at higher orders is increasingly an occupancy phenomenon.

**Temporal comparison (Fig. S3).** Between 1985 and 2015 the swamp gained 11 species and 35 individuals per quadrat on average whereas other habitats lost individuals. Its abundance-based T rose by 2.0 orders (0.7–0.8 elsewhere) and its LCBD fell, so it became less distinctive and more representative in abundance; its occurrence-based T rose by only 1.6 orders (3.5–4.7 elsewhere), losing its earlier above-average incidence retention (mean percentile 0.78 to 0.48). Abundance-based swamp quadrats remained near the bottom of the plot ranking (percentile 0.06 to 0.08). Quadrat-level patterns were moderately stable between censuses (ρ = 0.66 for LCBD, 0.63 for abundance-based T, 0.39 for occurrence-based T; within the swamp 0.75, 0.81 and 0.12).

## Discussion paragraph
Pairwise LCBD identifies the swamp quadrats as the most distinctive of the plot, as in Legendre and Condit (2019). The multisite local metrics add information that cannot be read from LCBD alone. First, in abundance data, characteristic retention is mostly a re-expression of pairwise distinctiveness; the additional information is in the conditional retention order, which shows that the shared composition of distinctive quadrats is retained more persistently across orders, as expected if the shared part is made of widespread dominant species. Second, with occurrence data, retention is essentially independent of LCBD: quadrats with indistinguishable LCBD differ by up to ten orders in how widely their species are shared, which reflects the occupancy distribution of their species. Third, the two representations disagree about where distinctive quadrats are at higher orders, and the abundance-based picture, driven by a few dominant species in unusual proportions, is the more persistent. Fourth, change over time can be decomposed: the swamp became less distinct in abundance but did not become more representative in occurrence, which pairwise distinctiveness cannot show. Caveats are that the swamp quadrats contain half as many individuals as other quadrats, so part of their distinctiveness may reflect sampling variance; that retention orders depend on the plot extent and quadrat size; and that regional representativeness is not conservation value or irreplaceability: a quadrat with low retention can be ordinary in its species pool, and a highly retentive quadrat can contain locally important species.

**Figure 4. Local compositional distinctiveness and regional representativeness in the Barro Colorado Island plot, 1985 census.** (A) Conventional local contribution to beta diversity (LCBD, Hellinger-transformed tree abundances) of the 1250 20 m × 20 m quadrats; orange outlines mark the 30 swamp quadrats and dots the quadrats with Holm-significant LCBD (99,999 permutations). (B) Characteristic retention order T based on occurrence (sum of the conditional zeta profile across all orders); dark quadrats retain shared composition across fewer orders. (C) Retention profiles (conditional shared composition against zeta order, log axis) of two swamp quadrats with almost identical LCBD (1.55 and 1.56 × 10⁻³) but occurrence-based T of 11.1 and 19.6; the grey band shows the regional median and 5–95% range. Relative-abundance profiles (left) are similar whereas occurrence profiles (right) diverge. (D) LCBD against characteristic retention order for relative abundance (left; Spearman ρ = −0.86) and occurrence (right; ρ = −0.03), coloured by habitat. Abundance-based T largely mirrors pairwise distinctiveness whereas occurrence-based T carries independent information; swamp quadrats lie below the regional trend in abundance-based retention.
